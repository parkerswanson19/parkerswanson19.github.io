Back in 2012 on the [EconTalk podcast](https://www.econtalk.org/coase-on-externalities-the-firm-and-the-state-of-economics/), Coase stated that he didn’t appreciate the “theorem” that came to bear his name<sup class="note-ref" tabindex="0" role="button" data-toggle="popover" data-trigger="hover focus" data-placement="top" title="Coase Theorem" data-content="When transaction costs are zero and property rights are clearly defined, parties can bargain to an efficient allocation regardless of who initially holds those rights.">1</sup>. Emerging from his 1960 paper [*The Problem of Social Cost*](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/466560), it elevated a hypothetical world without transaction costs over the much messier world in which they actually exist. In the paper itself, that frictionless world served as a benchmark; Coase’s broader concern was how law, rights, and institutions shape outcomes once the costs of bargaining are brought back in.

In theory, if bargaining were costless, the initial allocation of rights would not prevent the parties from reaching an efficient outcome on their own. But, as Coase put it, “... it’s an imaginary system. There always are transaction costs.” In the real world, those costs can prevent bargaining altogether, which is why Coase placed so much emphasis on the two-way nature of the problem<sup class="note-ref" tabindex="0" role="button" data-toggle="popover" data-trigger="hover focus" data-placement="top" title="Reciprocal harm" data-content="Harm is reciprocal: preventing A from harming B can itself impose a cost on A. For example, a factory may impose smoke or noise on nearby residents, but shutting it down also imposes costs on the factory, its workers, and its customers. The relevant question is not simply who is causing harm, but which arrangement creates the greater overall value.">2</sup> and on how the assignment and enforcement of rights can shape the resulting allocation of resources.<sup class="note-ref" tabindex="0" role="button" data-toggle="popover" data-trigger="hover focus" data-placement="top" title="Why the initial rule matters" data-content="When bargaining is costly, parties may be unable to negotiate around the initial legal rule. If a noisy business has the legal right to operate, nearby residents may simply bear the cost if negotiation is too expensive. If residents instead hold the right to quiet, the business may have to change its behavior. The initial assignment of rights can therefore affect which activities occur, at what scale, and who bears the associated costs.">3</sup>

Things feel a little different now than they did in 2012. AI agents, both enterprise<sup class="note-ref" tabindex="0" role="button" data-toggle="popover" data-trigger="click" data-html="true" data-placement="top" title="Enterprise agents" data-content="<a href='https://claude.com/solutions/enterprise'>Claude</a>, <a href='https://openai.com/business/workspace-agents/'>ChatGPT</a>, <a href='https://www.perplexity.ai/enterprise'>Perplexity</a>, and <a href='https://www.salesforce.com/agentforce/'>Agentforce</a>">4</sup> and personal<sup class="note-ref" tabindex="0" role="button" data-toggle="popover" data-trigger="click" data-html="true" data-placement="top" title="Personal agents" data-content="<a href='https://instinct.com/'>Instinct</a>, <a href='https://persona.chat/'>Persona</a>, <a href='https://gemini.google/overview/agent/spark/'>Gemini Spark</a>, and <a href='https://manus.im/'>Manus</a>">5</sup>, are flooding into the market, and when used well they can already remove meaningful amounts of everyday friction. An agent can search across thousands of options, compare terms, coordinate with services and other agents, and carry out a task that might otherwise require a long series of small decisions and interactions. And this is **just the beginning**. If agents continue reducing the cost of search, coordination, negotiation, and execution, the gap between Coase’s hypothetical world and our own begins to narrow.

What if the cost of transacting fell to little more than a handful of tokens? What if agents drove transaction costs so low that parties themselves could simply bargain around the legal ruling? What if private negotiation became so cheap that policy increasingly set only the starting point, not the final allocation of resources? What if the “imaginary” world described by Coase turned out to be real?
